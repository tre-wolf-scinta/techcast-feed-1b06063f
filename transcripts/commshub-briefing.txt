The Comms Hub, Explained

Liam: Welcome to a tour of the Comms Hub, the customer communications system Tre architected inside Salesforce. Here is the idea in one breath. Every email, text, and call the company sends to a customer runs through one set of rules, gets checked against one shared opt out list, and lands in one history on the customer's account. Today we will show you how it works by following one customer from the day they sign to the day their card fails. Bella, start us off with why this matters.

Bella: Happy to. Picture how customers hear from us today. At least eight sources send messages: an AI voice agent, the VanillaSoft dialer, the contact center phone system, a texting package in Salesforce, our own product, Zapier automations, scheduled Salesforce emails, and people sending by hand.

Four of those send texts, and each one honours a STOP reply only for itself. So a customer can text STOP to one sender and get a text from another the next morning. There is no single history, so understanding one customer means opening several systems. Teams avoid calling the same customer twice in one day because one operations lead coordinates it by hand. And who we contact is often a guess. On most accounts, the billing email is simply whoever signed the contract.

For the business, that means three risks: customers who feel spammed, opt outs we cannot prove we honoured, and contact data nobody can report on with confidence. The hub fixes all three. Liam, let's meet our customer.

Liam: Our example customer is made up: a heating and cooling company we will call Northside Heating. Maria owns it, and Kevin runs the office.

It starts at signing. The PandaDoc contract asks two new questions: where should invoices go, and who should we contact about a payment problem? Maria answers Kevin for invoices, and herself for payments. The hub turns those answers into contact roles. Kevin becomes the Invoice Recipient. Maria becomes the Payer, and, as the signer, the starting Decision Maker.

Here is the clever part. Every role also records how we know it. We call that its standing. From strongest to weakest, it can be verified by the customer, asserted by the customer, observed in a system, inferred as a best guess, or merely claimed. Right now Maria's and Kevin's roles are asserted, because they came from the contract.

A week later comes the welcome call. The Customer Success rep runs a Confirm roles step and walks through the roles with Maria on the call. Maria confirms them, and they become verified. From then on, whenever any program needs the Payer at Northside, a service called the recipient resolver answers with Maria, and says how it knows. When the resolver cannot find a solid answer, it says so, with the reason, instead of guessing. Bella, Kevin has something to say.

Bella: He does. A month in, Kevin gets an automated reminder text and replies STOP. In today's world, that STOP only reaches the tool that sent the text. In the hub, it reaches everything.

Here is how. Every email address and phone number becomes a contact point in Salesforce, and each contact point carries its opt ins and opt outs by purpose, such as billing, onboarding, or privacy policy. Kevin's STOP blocks automated texts and automated calls to his number everywhere, no matter which tool sent the original message. A person can still pick up the phone and call him.

Two design choices make this solid. First, consent is kept separate from roles. Being the Invoice Recipient does not mean Kevin agreed to texts. Second, whether a purpose needs an opt in before the first message is a switch in the rules, so the company can tighten it without a rebuild. That matters, because a federal rule expected in January twenty twenty seven would require one STOP to halt automated texts and calls across every channel. The shared opt out list is designed for exactly that. Liam, show them the engine.

Liam: Let's put Northside through a real program. Northside bought the experience product, and they cannot go live until their privacy policy is in place. So they enter the privacy policy outreach program.

A program is a set of rules in the rulebook: the steps, the timing, which role gets contacted, from which address or number, and what stops it. Northside now gets a conversation record, which tracks where they are in that program: the current step, when the next one is due, and any pause.

Several times an hour, a scheduled job we call the mailroom wakes up and runs five checks before anything goes out. One, is this still needed? If Northside already has its policy, it stops. Two, which step is due? Three, who exactly? The resolver says Maria. Four, is it allowed? Not opted out, not during quiet hours, and nobody else contacted Maria today. Five, what goes out, and how?

For this program, the first step is a call from the AI voice agent, which makes up to five attempts. If those do not reach Maria, the next step moves her to a manual list in VanillaSoft for a person to call. Anything that needs a human, like a call or removing access, becomes a regular Salesforce Task, so it shows up where people already work. Bella, where does all of this end up?

Bella: In my favourite part, the logbook. Every message in or out gets one row, whether the hub sent it, a connected tool sent it, or a person sent it by hand. Each row records what happened and why. Every voice agent attempt, every VanillaSoft call, Kevin's STOP, and even a send the hub decided to skip, with the reason.

So when someone opens Northside's account, they see the whole story in one place. And for reporting, that is gold: one clean, consistent table of every customer touch, across every team and tool.

A few rules keep the story tidy. Programs share one daily limit per address, so if two programs want to email Maria on the same day, the second email moves to the next business day. If Maria replies, the next automated email waits one business day. And nobody edits a conversation record directly. People and tools send a signal, like pause or stop, and the engine applies it, so every change is traceable. Liam, I believe Northside's card just failed.

Liam: It did. Months later, Northside's monthly charge fails in Stripe. A case opens in Salesforce, and within about half an hour it shows plain facts: the decline reason in plain words, whether Stripe will retry, and the open balance.

At first, the hub just watches. Stripe retries the card and emails Maria on its own for about three weeks, and a meaningful share of failed payments get paid with no other contact at all. That is why every hub message checks the balance first.

If Stripe gives up, or the decline is one no retry can fix, the collections program starts. The resolver finds the Payer, which is Maria. She gets an email from the billing team address, and any calls are handed to the collections specialist as tasks. The specialist steers the program with the case statuses she already uses. The moment the balance is paid, everything stops. And the hub never charges, refunds, or changes an account on its own. Those decisions stay with people. Bella, take us home on why it lasts.

Bella: The hub is vendor neutral. Programs name channels, like email or calls, never vendors. Each outside system, whether Stripe, VanillaSoft, or the texting package, sits behind one small piece of code called an adapter. Adding a simple vendor is estimated at about two days, and a complex one at up to a week. When billing moved to Stripe, the design held, and only one adapter changed.

New programs are easy to add too. A team fills in a short request form: which customers, which channels, how many contacts over how many days, and which role. Operations turns that into rules. Each department edits its own message wording, and timing changes go through a reviewed release, so every rule change is reviewed before it goes live.

Liam: So, the Comms Hub in three sentences. One engine in Salesforce runs every customer outreach program from one rulebook. Every message passes five checks and one shared opt out list, and lands in one logbook. And contact roles tell us who does what at each customer, and how sure we are.

For the business, that means fewer duplicate contacts, opt outs honoured everywhere, and customer communication data that is visible, clean, and ready to report on. Thanks for taking the tour with us.
